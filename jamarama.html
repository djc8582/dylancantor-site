Jamarama

Four phones, one shared clock, one fused track.

RoleAudio architecture and the jam system WithSai Nellutla AwardWinner, Audiotool Challenge — Google DeepMind Magenta Hackathon DateJune 2026

Two to four players each control one dimension of a single track from their phones — drums, harmony, lead, or crowd energy. When one player changes the chord, the whole band re-roots on the next downbeat.

  • Master clock lives in the host browser and fans beats to phones over WebSocket; phones are thin controllers.
  • The lead keyboard fires a local tone in under 20 ms, so playing never waits on a network round trip.
  • I measured the generative model for tempo stability first. It drifts within ~30 seconds — there is no tempo input — so I took it off the clock entirely: a deterministic clock-locked band wrapped in an arhythmic texture layer that floats off the grid, where drift is inaudible.
  • That layer runs Magenta RealTime 2 on-device via MLX at ~2.37× real-time, conditioned on blended player taste, live chord, and crowd energy.

The interesting constraint was never what the model could generate. It was what the model could generate in time — and that answer came from measuring rather than assuming.

What the texture layer is for

A clock-locked band of drums, chords and lead is tight, and it is thin. Four quantised parts, every onset landing exactly where you expect it, nothing in the gaps. What is missing is everything that makes a room sound occupied: density between the hits, timbre that does not repeat bar to bar, the sense that something is happening slightly off to one side of you.

That is the job the model does. It runs continuously underneath the band, conditioned on the blended taste of whoever is actually playing, the chord the harmony player is holding right now, and the crowd-energy dial — so the texture follows the performance instead of ignoring it. Change the chord and the layer moves with the band; push crowd energy and it thickens. It carries harmonic and timbral information but no transients anyone is counting on, which is exactly why it can add complexity without adding anything that is capable of being late.

Keeping it off the grid is what turns a defect into realism. On the grid, thirty seconds of drift is a band coming apart. Off the grid there is no downbeat for it to be late against, so the same drift reads as air in the room — a layer breathing against the beat rather than failing to hold it. Four people on four phones end up sounding like more than four parts, and the thing they are playing into sounds like a space rather than a sequencer.
